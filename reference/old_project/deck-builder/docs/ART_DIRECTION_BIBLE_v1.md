# Art Direction Bible v1

## Project Visual Identity

> **Quiet weathered fantasy. Monumental silhouettes, muted regional
> palettes, selective painterly detail, and atmosphere that consumes
> detail with distance.**

The game uses atmospheric 2D fantasy illustration built around strong
silhouettes, restrained painterly rendering, compressed color, selective
detail, and deep atmospheric perspective.

The world should evoke **solitude, scale, age, mystery, beauty, and
melancholy**. The baseline identity is not strictly dark fantasy. The
constant is the feeling of traveling through a vast, old world that
exists far beyond the player.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 1. Shape Before Detail

Every major environmental element should work first as a silhouette.

Favor forms that are slightly exaggerated, irregular, asymmetric,
elongated where appropriate, and deliberately composed. Realism is
subordinate to visual design.

A tree should be recognizable by its gesture before its bark is visible.
A building should have an interesting mass before windows or ornament
are added. A mountain should have a memorable profile before rock
texture is painted.

## 2. Rendering Style

Use **restrained painterly illustration** rather than photorealism or
flat vector artwork.

Surfaces should contain enough information to suggest their material
without describing every physical feature.

**Large shape -\> secondary shape -\> selective texture**

Avoid uniform micro-detail across the image.

## 3. Color and Chroma

Color should generally remain **muted and low-chroma**, without becoming
monochrome.

Colors within a scene should feel influenced by the same atmospheric
color. Avoid pure, highly saturated colors unless deliberately reserved
for a rare focal purpose.

### Regional Palette Families

  Region                     Possible Palette Family
  -------------------------- -------------------------------------
  Coast                      Blue-gray, slate, muted teal
  Highland                   Ochre, gray-green, stone
  Forest                     Moss, muted emerald, blue-green
  Snow                       Pale blue, lavender-gray, charcoal
  Autumn                     Dusty amber, brown, muted red
  Strange / magical region   Desaturated violet, mauve, gray
  Underground                Mineral blue, charcoal, muted earth

Consistency comes from **how color is treated**, not from using the same
hue everywhere.

## 4. Atmospheric Perspective

> **Distance removes information.**

As environmental elements recede:

-   Detail decreases
-   Contrast decreases
-   Saturation decreases
-   Edge sharpness decreases
-   Texture decreases
-   Color variation decreases

Extreme-distance elements may become little more than pale silhouettes
merging with the atmospheric color.

Atmosphere should feel capable of **consuming the world**, rather than
simply appearing as transparent fog placed over otherwise detailed
scenery.

Fog is not mandatory. The same principle can be achieved through haze,
rain, sea spray, snow, dust, humidity, smoke, bright air, darkness, or
other environmental conditions.

## 5. Value Hierarchy

Contrast should primarily separate **depth planes**, not every
individual object.

**Foreground framing:** darkest values, strong silhouettes, limited
internal information.

**Gameplay plane:** dark-to-middle values, clear traversal geometry,
sufficient separation for character readability.

**Near background:** reduced contrast, texture, and detail.

**Far background:** softer edges, lower contrast, lighter atmospheric
values.

**Extreme distance:** minimal detail; shapes may nearly merge into
atmospheric illumination.

Avoid giving every rock, brick, plant, or surface its own high-contrast
definition.

## 6. Lighting

Default lighting should favor **diffuse environmental illumination**:
overcast skies, haze, mist, reflected environmental light, and soft
ambient illumination.

The atmosphere itself may sometimes appear faintly luminous without an
obvious directional source.

Strong cinematic lighting may be used when narratively appropriate, but
should not become the default. Avoid constant reliance on god rays,
strong rim lighting, dramatic sunsets, extreme directional spotlights,
or excessive cinematic contrast.

## 7. Shadows

Avoid pure black as the default shadow color.

Dark areas should retain a relationship with the regional palette:
blue-charcoal, green-black, brown-gray, violet-charcoal, etc.

Foreground silhouettes may approach black but should often preserve
faint internal information.

## 8. Detail Distribution

Detail must be **hierarchical and selective**.

-   **High detail:** important nearby objects, focal points, gameplay
    landmarks, selected material definition.
-   **Medium detail:** typical gameplay-space environment.
-   **Low detail:** background forms.
-   **Extremely low detail:** distant atmospheric layers.

Do not distribute detail evenly across the image.

**Negative space is intentional composition, not unfinished artwork.**

## 9. Environmental Scale

The player character should frequently feel small relative to the world.

Large-scale forms are encouraged: ancient trees, cliffs, mountains,
walls, towers, bridges, caverns, ships, statues, geological formations,
and monumental interiors.

Scale does not require complexity. One enormous, memorable silhouette
can be more effective than dozens of small objects.

## 10. Side-Scroller Composition

Traversal scenes should generally be conceived as:

**Foreground framing -\> Gameplay plane -\> Near background -\> Far
background -\> Atmosphere / sky**

These layers have different visual responsibilities, not merely
different parallax speeds.

The traversable surface must remain visually understandable. The player
should intuitively understand: **I can walk here.**

Avoid artificial-looking glowing outlines or conspicuously game-like
platform indicators unless they belong to the established world
language.

## 11. Character Integration

The player character should remain one of the cleaner visual elements in
the frame.

Against the area directly behind the character:

-   Avoid excessive texture
-   Maintain sufficient value separation
-   Avoid strong competing silhouettes
-   Preserve the character's readable outline

Character and environment share the principles of **simplification,
silhouette, restrained color, and selective detail**.

## 12. Environmental Shape Language

Nature and architecture should feel **designed rather than merely
observed**.

A real tree may contain hundreds of branches; the game tree needs the
few branches that create the strongest gesture. A real ruined wall may
contain thousands of stones; the game wall needs enough information to
communicate old masonry.

Prioritize composition and memorable form over literal simulation.

## 13. Architecture

The game's architectural identity is **not inherently Gothic**.

Gothic ruins demonstrated useful properties: verticality, strong
silhouettes, repetition, monumental scale, negative space, and
architectural framing of distant layers. These principles may be
translated into completely different architectural languages.

Different regions or civilizations may develop their own proportions,
geometry, ornamentation, materials, construction logic, and silhouettes.

## 14. Nature

Nature should follow the same simplification principles as architecture.

-   Grass becomes grouped masses and directional strokes
-   Leaves become clusters
-   Rock becomes broad planes
-   Mountains become silhouettes
-   Clouds become broad value structures
-   Forest canopies become designed masses

Only selected areas receive finer rendering. Avoid turning natural
environments into highly detailed realistic landscape paintings.

## 15. Warm Accents

Warm color is allowed but should not become a formula.

Avoid making every environment **cool blue + orange torch**.

Warm accents become more powerful when they have contextual meaning: an
inhabited window, campfire, sunlight breaking through cloud, settlement
at night, autumn field, or character/combat effect.

Some scenes should contain no warm accent at all.

## 16. Weather and Atmosphere

Weather is an important tool for mood and depth but should vary by
region.

Possible treatments include fog, rain, sea spray, snow, dust, wind,
cloud cover, humidity, ash, pollen, smoke, bright haze, darkness, and
clear air.

The purpose is atmosphere and depth, not making every region foggy.

## 17. Regional Expression

### Consistent Across the Game

-   Silhouette-first design
-   Selective detail
-   Restrained saturation
-   Atmospheric depth
-   Strong value hierarchy
-   Designed environmental forms
-   Painterly simplification
-   Character readability
-   Monumental scale where appropriate

### Allowed to Change Dramatically

-   Hue
-   Weather
-   Architecture
-   Vegetation
-   Terrain
-   Lighting conditions
-   Environmental density
-   Civilization level
-   Amount of decay
-   Mood
-   Time of day

Regional variation should be substantial while preserving the underlying
visual grammar.

## 18. Anti-Goals

Actively avoid:

-   Photorealism
-   Generic AAA fantasy concept-art rendering
-   Hyper-detailed surfaces everywhere
-   Excessively crisp distant scenery
-   Oversaturated fantasy colors
-   Teal/orange grading as a default
-   Pure-black shadows throughout the scene
-   Constant dramatic directional lighting
-   Constant fog
-   Constant ruins
-   Constant Gothic architecture
-   Constant visual clutter
-   Tiny details replacing strong composition
-   Perfectly realistic architecture and geology
-   Excessive foreground foliage
-   Filling empty space simply because it exists
-   Giving every scene a glowing focal point
-   Allowing characters to disappear against busy backgrounds

> **Do not equate visual complexity with visual quality.**

## 19. Current Visual Anchors

### Anchor A --- Original Ruins

Primary reference for atmosphere, value compression, low chroma,
monumental scale, architectural layering, luminous distance, and
information loss through atmospheric depth. This remains the primary
visual north star.

### Anchor B --- Beach Gameplay Test

Primary reference for translating the visual identity into a
horizontally traversable gameplay screen, foreground/gameplay/background
separation, character scale, coastal regional palette, and side-scroller
readability.

### Anchor C --- Highland Test

Primary reference for regional flexibility, natural landscapes, non-teal
palette families, brighter environments that retain melancholy,
monumental natural silhouettes, and proof that the style does not depend
on Gothic architecture or ruins.

## 20. AI Generation Guidance

> **Prioritize large readable silhouettes and atmospheric depth over
> surface detail. Render with restrained painterly simplification, low
> chroma, compressed local contrast, diffuse environmental light, and
> progressively reduced information with distance. Use a coherent but
> region-specific muted palette. Preserve negative space and clear depth
> planes. Avoid photorealism, generic cinematic concept-art rendering,
> excessive texture, oversaturation, and unnecessary visual clutter.**

Subject-specific prompts should add location, terrain, architecture,
weather, mood, gameplay requirements, and regional palette **after**
this core visual language rather than replacing it.

## 21. Production Checklist

When evaluating environment art, ask:

1.  Does the composition work through large shapes alone?
2.  Is the gameplay plane immediately readable?
3.  Does the player character remain legible?
4.  Does distance visibly remove information?
5.  Is the regional palette cohesive and restrained?
6.  Is detail concentrated where it matters?
7.  Does the scene contain enough negative space?
8.  Does the environment feel larger than the character?
9.  Does the image feel illustrated rather than photorealistic?
10. Does it belong to the same visual world as the current anchor
    images?

If a piece fails the early questions, adding more detail is unlikely to
fix it.
